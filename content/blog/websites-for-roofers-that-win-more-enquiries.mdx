Most roofing work still comes from word of mouth, repeat customers and the van parked outside a job. That matters, but it isn't the whole picture any more. When a homeowner spots a slipped tile after a storm, or a landlord needs a flat roof sorted before a tenancy starts, the first thing they often do is search online, usually on a mobile, usually in a hurry to find someone local who looks like they know what they're doing. Good websites for roofers are built with exactly that moment in mind.

A site that's slow to load, vague about what you actually cover, or missing a clear way to get in touch will cost you enquiries you never even know you lost. The good news is that fixing this is straightforward once you know what to look for.

## Websites for roofers: what actually brings in enquiries

Many roofing websites were built years ago, often by a relative or a cheap template service, and haven't been touched since. They tend to share the same faults: slow to load on a phone, written around general 'roofing services' rather than the specific jobs people search for, and filled with stock photography that could belong to any trade in any town.

Think about how specific a search can be. Someone with a damaged chimney stack doesn't search for 'roofer', they search for 'chimney repair near me' or 'leadwork repair'. If your site never mentions leadwork, chimneys, flat roofs or guttering as distinct services, it's much harder for that search to find you, and even if someone does land on your homepage, they won't know you do that work unless it's spelled out.

The other common problem is friction at the point of contact. A long enquiry form asking for a full project description before someone has even spoken to you puts people off. Most homeowners with a leak want to speak to someone quickly, not fill in a questionnaire.

## How a good roofer website works, step by step

A website that actually brings in work tends to follow a simple pattern:

1. Someone searches for a specific job and a specific area, such as 'flat roof repair' or 're-roof quote' plus their town.
2. They land on a page that loads quickly on mobile data, because a lot of roofing enquiries happen from a van, a site, or a kitchen table with patchy signal.
3. The page clearly states what you do, where you cover, and shows proof you've done similar work before.
4. They see reviews or testimonials that reassure them you're reliable and tidy.
5. They get in touch through whichever method suits them: a quick call, a WhatsApp message, or a short enquiry form.
6. You, or an automated system, respond fast enough that they don't ring the next roofer on the list.

That last step matters more than most roofers realise. A website can generate the enquiry, but if nobody replies for two days, the job often goes elsewhere. ## What good looks like for a roofing business

A roofing website that pulls its weight usually has a few things in common:

- The homepage states the trade, the area covered and the main services within the first few seconds of someone landing on it.
- There are separate pages, or at least separate clear sections, for roof repairs, re-roofs, flat roofs, guttering, chimney and leadwork, and fascias and soffits, each written for what that customer actually needs to know.
- Photos are real completed jobs, not stock imagery. A gallery showing an actual re-roof or a tidy guttering job does more to build trust than any amount of text.
- Reviews are visible, not just linked off to another site. People read two or three before deciding whether to bother contacting anyone.
- The areas covered are named specifically, listing the towns and postcodes you work in rather than a vague claim to be 'local'.
- Contact options are simple: a click-to-call number, a WhatsApp link, and a short enquiry form that only asks for what's needed to get the conversation started.
- The whole thing loads quickly and reads well on a small screen, since most roofing enquiries happen on mobile.

## Common mistakes roofers make with their website

A few patterns come up again and again:

- Treating the website as a one-off job: built once, never revisited, with no new photos added as jobs are completed.
- Burying every service under a single generic page instead of giving roof repairs, re-roofs, flat roofs, guttering, and chimney and leadwork their own space to answer the questions people actually have.
- Using stock photography of roofs that look nothing like the work actually being done, which undermines trust the moment a sceptical visitor notices.
- Leaving out specific towns and areas covered, which makes it harder both for search engines and for people to work out if you cover their postcode.
- Asking for too much information upfront in the enquiry form, which puts off people who just want a quick quote for guttering or a fascia repair.
- No visible reviews, despite plenty of happy customers who'd have left one if asked.
- A site that looks fine on a desktop but is slow or awkward on a phone, which is where most enquiries actually come from.

## How to tell if your website is actually working

It's easy to assume a website is doing its job just because it exists. The only real way to know is to track what happens on it.

Start by asking new customers how they found you. If 'I looked you up online' comes up often, the website is pulling weight. If it never comes up, something is missing, whether that's visibility in search results or enough reason to get in touch once someone lands there.

Look at the basics: how fast the site loads on a phone, whether the enquiry form actually works, and whether the areas and services listed match what you want to be found for. If you run any paid advertising alongside the site, compare how many visitors turn into enquiries. A healthy roofing website should convert a reasonable share of visitors into calls, messages or form fills, not just rack up visits with nothing to show for it.

### Do I need a separate page for every roofing service?

It helps. A page dedicated to flat roofs, for example, can answer the specific questions someone has about that job and show relevant photos, which tends to work better than one page trying to cover roof repairs, re-roofs, guttering and leadwork all at once.

### Can a website actually help with emergency repair enquiries?

Yes, provided it's set up to make contact easy and fast. A clear phone number, a WhatsApp link and a short enquiry form all help someone with an urgent leak get in touch quickly rather than scrolling for ages looking for a way to reach you.

### How often should a roofing website be updated?

Regularly enough that the photos and areas covered stay accurate. Adding recent completed jobs, whether that's a re-roof, a guttering job or some chimney leadwork, keeps the site looking active and gives visitors fresh proof of the standard of work.
