Most electricians get work from word of mouth, Google and a handful of trade apps. The website, if there is one, sits quietly in the background. Nobody really looks at it until a customer mentions they couldn't find your opening hours, or a landlord asks for a link to your EICR pricing and you realise there isn't one. Websites for electricians don't need to be flashy, but they do need to do a specific job: turn someone with a tripped consumer unit, a new EV charger to fit, or a block of flats needing EICRs into a booked enquiry.

A lot of electrician websites were built years ago, by a relative or a cheap template service, and never touched again. They look dated on a phone, they don't say which areas are covered, and they list 'electrical services' without mentioning rewires, consumer unit upgrades, EV charger installs or fault finding. That vagueness costs you work, because the customer comparing three electricians' sites at 9pm usually enquires with whoever looks like they know exactly what the job involves.

## The problem with most electrician websites

A few patterns show up again and again on electrical trade websites.

- **No clear list of services.** A homepage that just says 'domestic and commercial electrical work' doesn't help someone searching for an EV charger installer or landlord EICR checks decide you're the right fit.
- **No areas covered.** If you work across several towns, say so. People searching on their phone want to know quickly whether you'll travel to them.
- **Slow on mobile.** Most enquiries start on a phone, often from someone standing next to a dead socket or a tripped consumer unit. A site that loads slowly, or has text too small to read without zooming, loses people before they've read a word.
- **No real photos.** Stock images of generic wiring diagrams don't build trust the way photos of your own consumer unit installs, EV charger setups or finished lighting jobs do.
- **Buried contact details.** A phone number hidden in a footer, or a long contact form, puts people off at the exact moment they were ready to get in touch.

None of this is really about design. It's about whether the site answers the questions a customer actually has: can you do this job, do you cover my area, and how do I reach you right now.

## How a good electrician website works, step by step

A website built to win enquiries usually follows a similar structure, whatever trade it's for. For an electrician, that looks like this.

1. **A homepage that states the obvious clearly.** Who you are, what you do (rewires, consumer units, EV chargers, EICRs, lighting, fault finding), and where you work, readable in the first few seconds without scrolling.
2. **Service pages for the jobs that bring in work.** A dedicated page for EICRs explaining what a landlord needs and how often one's required does more for your enquiries than a paragraph buried on a general page. The same applies to EV charger installs, which often raise questions about charger types and timescales.
3. **An areas covered page or section.** Listing the towns you work in helps both the customer and search engines understand where you operate.
4. **Real photos and reviews.** Photos of actual consumer unit upgrades, EV charger installs and finished lighting work, alongside genuine reviews, build far more trust than copy alone.
5. **An obvious, low-friction way to get in touch.** A clickable phone number, a short enquiry form, or a WhatsApp link. Fewer steps between 'I need an electrician' and 'I've sent a message' means more enquiries converted.
6. **Fast loading and clean navigation.** Nobody should have to pinch and zoom to find your number or your prices.

## Websites for electricians: what good looks like

A genuinely good electrician website reads like it was written by someone who understands the work, not a generic template. That means naming the actual jobs people search for: rewires for older housing stock, consumer unit upgrades to meet current regulations, EV charger installs for homeowners switching to electric vehicles, EICRs for landlords renewing a tenancy, and fault finding for the socket that's stopped working or the light that keeps flickering.

It also means being specific about who the work is for. A landlord searching for EICR services wants reassurance about certification and renewal timelines. A homeowner researching an EV charger wants to understand the installation process and how long it takes. Separate sections for domestic and landlord work make it easier for each customer to find what they need quickly.

Good sites also make trust visible rather than implied: real photos of consumer units you've fitted, EV chargers mounted on actual houses, and lighting installs in finished rooms, plus genuine reviews rather than a vague claim about being trusted. And the site needs to be fast and simple on a phone, because that's where most enquiries start, whether that's a homeowner after a late-night fault or a landlord booking an EICR before a deadline.

## Common mistakes electricians make with their websites

- **Treating the website as a brochure, not a tool for enquiries.** If there's no clear next step on every page, the site is only doing half a job.
- **Listing every possible service vaguely instead of the ones that bring in work.** Rewires, consumer units, EV chargers, EICRs, lighting and fault finding each deserve a clear mention.
- **Ignoring landlords as a separate audience.** EICR work often comes from letting agents and landlords with multiple properties. A site that only speaks to homeowners misses this entirely.
- **Letting the site go stale.** Old phone numbers, missing mentions of EV charger work, or outdated information all chip away at trust.
- **No link between the website and how enquiries actually arrive.** A contact form that emails an inbox nobody checks means enquiries sit unanswered while the customer moves on.

This last point matters more than most electricians expect. A site that generates an enquiry on a Sunday evening is only useful if something happens with it quickly. Some electricians pair their site with an [AI chatbot](/blog/ai-chatbot-for-electricians) that replies instantly to website visitors, qualifies the basics (job type, location, timing) and gets the details through before the customer thinks about calling someone else.

## How to judge whether your website is working

The simplest test is whether the site brings in enquiries you'd actually want, not just visits, but messages, calls and bookings for the jobs you want more of.

A few practical checks:

- Open your own site on your phone and time how long it takes to load.
- Search for your own services plus your area, and see whether you appear and how your site compares to competitors'.
- Ask a recent customer how they found you and what nearly put them off.
- Track where enquiries actually come from over a month. If the website isn't contributing, something in the structure above is probably missing.

If you're getting traffic but few enquiries, the fix is usually about clarity and friction rather than a full redesign: clearer service pages, an easier way to get in touch, or faster load times. If you're getting enquiries but they're often the wrong fit, clearer service and area pages help people self-select before they contact you.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a website for an electrician cost?

Costs vary depending on how many service pages you need, whether you want booking or quote forms built in, and how much content you already have. The [web design pricing](/web-design/pricing) page sets out how projects are typically structured.

### Do I need separate pages for EICRs, EV chargers and rewires?

It helps. Each brings in a different type of customer with different questions, so separate pages, or at least clearly separated sections, make it easier for each visitor to find what they need and get in touch.

### Will a new website actually bring in more work on its own?

A well built site makes it easier for people already looking for an electrician to find you and get in touch, and it supports any advertising or referrals you run. A slow, unclear or outdated site will lose enquiries that should have been straightforward.

### Should I list every area I cover, or just my main town?

List every area you genuinely work in, including nearby towns. People searching for an electrician usually search locally, and a site mentioning only one town can look like it doesn't cover areas you actually do.

### What's the difference between a website and a full lead generation setup?

A website is the destination: it needs to load fast, explain your services clearly and make it easy to get in touch. A full [lead generation](/services/lead-generation) setup adds paid ads, instant lead forms and automated follow-up on top of that website, so enquiries are captured without you watching your phone constantly.
