Most landscaping businesses still rely on a mix of word of mouth, a board outside the last job, and a Facebook page that gets the occasional like. That works well enough when you're busy, but it leaves you exposed the moment a few regular customers dry up. Facebook ads for landscapers solve a specific problem: they put your patios, decking, turfing and fencing work in front of homeowners in your area who are actively thinking about a garden project, rather than waiting for them to stumble across your page.

The appeal is obvious. The harder part is making ads that bring in people who are genuinely ready to spend money on a garden, rather than a flood of messages asking for free advice. That difference comes down to how the ads are built and where the enquiry lands, not just how much you spend.

## Why landscapers struggle to get consistent enquiries

Garden work is seasonal and visual, which makes it well suited to ads on Facebook and Instagram, but also means a lot of landscapers get this wrong. A generic boosted post showing a nice patio might get likes and comments, but likes don't book jobs. Without a clear next step, people scroll past, admire the photo, and forget about it by teatime.

There's also the seasonal pressure. Enquiries for turfing and planting peak in spring, decking and patios pick up through early summer, and fencing often spikes after winter storms. If your only source of new work is referrals, you have no way to smooth out those gaps or pull enquiries forward before the busy season even starts.

The other common issue is that a lot of garden enquiries aren't real jobs at all. Someone wants a rough idea of what a patio 'might cost' with no measurements, no budget in mind, and no real intention of booking anyone soon. Chasing these eats into evenings that should be spent quoting proper jobs.

## How Facebook ads for landscapers actually work, step by step

A well-built campaign follows a fairly simple structure, even though the targeting behind it is doing a lot of work.

1. **Ads are shown to people in your actual working area.** Rather than running ads to the whole country, they're geo-targeted to the towns and postcodes you cover, so you're not paying to reach someone two hours away who will never book you.
2. **The creative matches a specific job type.** A photo or short video of a finished patio, a newly turfed lawn, or a run of fencing performs far better than a generic 'we do landscaping' graphic, because it shows exactly what someone can expect.
3. **The click goes to an instant lead form, not a website.** Facebook's native lead forms open inside the app, load instantly, and ask only for the details that matter: name, phone number, postcode, and a line or two on the job (for example, 'patio, roughly 30 square metres, want to start this spring').
4. **The enquiry arrives with the information you need to follow up properly.** Instead of a vague message saying 'interested, can you call me', you get a job type, a rough location and a sense of timing before you've even picked up the phone.
5. **Follow-up happens fast.** Many landscapers lose jobs simply because they're up a ladder or mid-job when the enquiry comes in, and by the time they call back that evening, the homeowner has already booked someone else. Speed of response matters as much as the ad itself.

That last point is why some landscaping businesses pair Facebook ads with an automated first reply, usually over WhatsApp, that confirms the enquiry, asks a couple of qualifying questions and gets a rough job booked into the calendar within minutes rather than hours. ## Facebook ads for landscapers: what good targeting and creative look like

Good campaigns for landscaping businesses tend to share a few traits.

- **The area is realistic.** A targeting radius based on how far you're actually willing to travel for a job, not an entire county, keeps enquiries local and relevant.
- **Each ad focuses on one job type.** A separate ad for decking, another for patios, another for fencing, each with its own photos and its own lead form questions, performs better than one ad trying to cover everything you do.
- **The photos are your own work.** Stock images of perfect show gardens look out of place next to real driveways and real British weather. A genuine before-and-after of a patio or a newly laid lawn builds more trust than a polished stock shot ever will.
- **The lead form asks for the right details.** For a patio or decking enquiry, that might mean rough size, current surface, and whether they have a design in mind. For turfing or planting, it might mean plot size and whether it's a full garden refit or a smaller area.
- **Budget ranges are set honestly.** Giving people a rough starting price range in the ad (even a broad one) filters out people who were never going to spend what the job actually costs, and saves you quoting jobs that go nowhere.

## Common mistakes landscapers make with Facebook ads

A few patterns come up again and again when garden businesses try running ads themselves.

- **Sending every click to the website homepage.** A homepage is built to explain who you are, not to capture a job enquiry in ten seconds. Every extra click and every second of loading time loses people, especially on mobile.
- **Running one ad for everything you do.** A single ad trying to sell patios, decking, turfing, fencing and planting in one go rarely performs as well as several focused ads, because the photo and the message can't speak clearly to any one of them.
- **Switching the ad off after a quiet week.** Garden projects often get discussed and planned over several weeks before someone commits, so judging an ad on seven days of results, especially outside peak season, tends to lead to switching off something that was about to start working.
- **Ignoring the follow-up.** An ad can do its job perfectly and still fail to turn into booked work if enquiries sit unanswered for a day. Fast, consistent follow-up matters more than most landscapers expect.
- **No clear budget guidance in the ad.** Without at least a rough steer on price, you'll attract people wanting a full garden transformation on a budget that would barely cover turfing.

## How to judge whether your Facebook ads are working

It's easy to focus on vanity numbers like reach or likes, but those don't pay for materials or wages. The numbers that actually matter are:

- **Cost per enquiry.** What you're paying, on average, for each lead form submission. This varies by job type and area, but tracking it over time tells you whether targeting or creative changes are helping.
- **Enquiry to quote ratio.** Of the people who enquire, how many turn into an actual site visit or quote. If this ratio is low, the targeting or the ad message probably needs adjusting rather than the budget increasing.
- **Quote to booked job ratio.** This tells you whether the enquiries arriving are genuinely ready to commit, or whether they're still at the 'just looking' stage.
- **Response time.** How quickly enquiries get a reply. If leads sit for hours, that alone can explain a poor conversion rate regardless of how good the ads are.
- **Seasonal patterns.** Tracking performance month by month shows you when to increase spend ahead of your busy season and when to ease off, rather than running the same budget all year round.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should a landscaping business spend on Facebook ads?

There's no fixed figure that suits every business, since it depends on your area, how competitive it is, and the job types you're chasing. It's more useful to start with a modest, testable budget, track cost per enquiry closely, and increase spend once you know the numbers work for your business rather than guessing at a figure upfront.

### Do Facebook ads work for garden design and high-end landscaping, not just patios and turfing?

Yes, though the approach differs slightly. Higher-value garden design work tends to need more detailed creative, often video or a short portfolio carousel, and a lead form that asks more qualifying questions, since the sales cycle is usually longer than a straightforward patio or fencing job.

### Is it better to run ads all year or just in spring and summer?

Many landscaping businesses do best running ads year round at a lower budget through the quieter months, then increasing spend ahead of peak season. This keeps your name in front of people who are planning ahead, such as those thinking about a new patio for next summer, while avoiding wasted spend chasing turfing enquiries in the depths of winter.

### What's wrong with just sending clicks to my website instead of a lead form?

Nothing is wrong with having a good website, but a website requires several extra clicks and page loads before someone can actually enquire. A native lead form loads instantly inside Facebook or Instagram and captures the details you need in one short step, which tends to bring in more enquiries from the same ad spend.

### Can I run these ads myself or do I need help setting them up?

It's possible to run basic ads yourself, but getting the targeting, lead form questions and follow-up process right takes some trial and error.
