Most fencing contractors get work through word of mouth, referrals and boards left up after a job. A website often feels like an afterthought, something a nephew built years ago or a template nobody has touched since. But when someone's fence blows down in a storm, or a new-build homeowner wants garden fencing and a gate before they move in, the first thing many of them do is search on their phone. Good websites for fencing contractors are what turn that search into a booked job rather than a missed enquiry.

This matters more for fencing than for some other trades because the work is visual. A customer wants to see what a close-board fence with concrete posts actually looks like finished, not just read a paragraph describing it. They want to know you cover their postcode, that you can turn a storm repair around quickly, and that someone will actually answer when they get in touch.

## Why most fencing contractor websites lose work before the phone rings

A lot of fencing websites make the same mistakes. Stock photos of fencing that doesn't match what the business actually installs. A single 'contact us' page with no phone number visible on mobile. No mention of which towns or postcodes are covered, so the visitor isn't sure if it's worth asking. No reviews, so there's nothing to reassure a stranger that the quote will be fair and the job will get finished properly.

None of this is unusual. Most small trade businesses build a website once, early on, and never revisit it as the business grows. The problem is that fencing work is often urgent or time-sensitive. Someone with a fence panel flat on the lawn after high winds isn't going to scroll through five pages looking for a phone number. They'll leave and call the next result instead.

The fix isn't a bigger website. It's a website built around how people actually search and decide, with the right information in the right place.

## How websites for fencing contractors should be built, step by step

A website that does its job for a fencing business tends to follow the same basic structure, whatever the design looks like on top.

1. **A clear homepage that states what you do and where.** Garden fencing, gates, commercial fencing, storm damage repairs, and the towns or radius you cover. This should be readable in the first few seconds, not buried under a slogan.
2. **Separate pages or sections for each main job type.** A homeowner looking for a new garden fence and a landlord dealing with storm damage are in different moods and want different reassurance. Splitting these out, even briefly, helps both find what they need faster.
3. **Real photos of completed jobs.** Close-board, panel, post and rail, gates, commercial hoarding, whatever you actually install. Before and after shots work particularly well for storm damage repairs, since they show speed and competence in one image.
4. **Reviews or testimonials placed near the point of decision**, not hidden on a separate page nobody visits.
5. **An obvious way to get in touch on every page**, ideally a phone number that's tappable on mobile, plus a short enquiry form for people who prefer not to call.
6. **Fast loading on a mobile signal, not just office wifi.** Anyone searching from a garden or a van on site is often on patchy 4G.

## What good websites for fencing contractors look like

A good fencing website reads less like a brochure and more like a straightforward answer to the questions a visitor already has in their head. Do you cover my area? Do you do the type of job I need? What does your work actually look like finished? Can I trust you?

In practice that means:

- The areas covered are named explicitly, not just 'we cover the local area'. A visitor searching from a specific postcode wants to see their town or nearby ones mentioned.
- Job types are separated clearly: garden fencing and gates for homeowners, storm damage repairs presented as a fast-response service, commercial fencing shown with different examples and language aimed at site managers or landlords.
- Photos are recent, taken on site, and show a range of fence styles and finishes rather than one lucky shot repeated everywhere.
- Pricing is handled honestly. Most fencing jobs need a site visit to quote properly, so the site should say that plainly rather than pretending an instant online price is possible.
- The whole thing loads quickly and looks right on a phone screen, since that's where most enquiries start.

A site with all of this doesn't need to be flashy. It needs to be clear, current and quick to act on.

## Common mistakes fencing contractors make with their website

The same handful of issues come up repeatedly:

- **Treating storm damage repairs the same as routine garden fencing.** Someone dealing with a fence down after a storm wants speed and reassurance above everything else. Burying that service under a general 'services' page slows them down at exactly the wrong moment.
- **No mention of commercial work**, even when the business does it. Commercial fencing enquiries often come through a slightly different route, and a site with no trace of that work won't turn up when a facilities manager or developer searches for it.
- **Letting the site go stale.** Photos from years ago, prices that changed, areas covered that have expanded. A visitor comparing a few contractors will notice if a site feels out of date.
- **Making contact harder than it needs to be.** A contact form that asks for ten fields before someone can send a simple enquiry will lose people who are on their phone in a hurry.
- **No social proof at all.** Fencing work involves someone's garden, boundary or business premises. Reviews from previous customers do a lot of the reassurance work that a salesperson would otherwise have to do in person.

If any of these sound familiar, it's worth an honest look at the current site before spending money on adverts or marketing to drive more traffic to it.

## How to tell if your website is actually working

It's easy to assume a website is fine simply because it exists and looks reasonably tidy. The better test is whether it's actually producing enquiries.

Worth checking:

- How many enquiries come through the site each month, and from which pages.
- Whether visitors on mobile are dropping off before reaching a contact method.
- Whether enquiries mention the specific service they searched for, which suggests the relevant page matched what they were looking for.
- How quickly enquiries get a response. A well-built site that then sits unanswered for a day loses much of its value, since fencing enquiries, particularly storm damage ones, often go to whoever replies first.

Some fencing contractors pair the website with paid advertising and automated follow-up so that enquiries get an instant response even outside working hours, similar to the approach covered in [how driveway contractors get more driveway leads](/blog/how-driveway-contractors-get-more-driveway-leads-in-fleetwood), which works on the same principle of speed and clarity turning a visitor into a booked job. A website is the foundation either way. Without it working properly, extra traffic just means more people leaving without getting in touch.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a website for a fencing contractor cost?

Costs vary depending on how many pages you need, whether you want separate sections for domestic and commercial work, and how much content and photography needs producing. The [web design pricing](/web-design/pricing) page sets out typical costs so you can weigh it against the enquiries it's likely to bring in.

### Do I need separate pages for garden fencing, gates and commercial fencing?

It helps. These jobs attract different customers with different concerns, and separating them means each visitor lands on content that speaks directly to what they need, rather than a general page that tries to cover everything at once.

### Should storm damage repairs be treated differently on the website?

Yes. These enquiries are urgent and the customer is often stressed. A clear, fast-loading page that states you handle emergency repairs, with a visible phone number and quick response times, will convert far better than folding it into a general services list.

### How many photos do I actually need?

Enough to show the range of work you do: a handful of garden fencing jobs, a gate or two, a storm repair before and after, and commercial fencing if that's part of the business. Quality and recency matter more than quantity.

### Can a website work alongside paid adverts and lead generation?

Yes, and it generally works better that way. Adverts drive people to the site, and the site then needs to convert them quickly with clear information and an obvious way to get in touch. Without a solid site behind them, adverts often end up paying to send visitors somewhere they immediately leave.
